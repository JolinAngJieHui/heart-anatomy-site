## Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is characterized by myocardial necrosis resulting from an acute occlusion of a coronary artery. Myocardial salvage by immediate reperfusion therapy is critical to limit myocardial necrosis. It is known that a short time from onset to reperfusion of an occluded coronary artery is associated with better clinical outcomes [1]. In daily practice, physicians sometimes encounter patients with AMI whose initial electrocardiogram (ECG) and cardiac biomarkers are subtle and nondiagnostic. In such cases, diagnosing AMI with subsequent reperfusion therapy could be delayed.

## Introduction

It is known that ECG changes are sometimes subtle in high lateral wall myocardial infarction [2]. This is because there are no leads that directly show this region, and the distance between the electrodes and this region is relatively far, resulting in small potential changes that are less noticeable in an ECG.

## Introduction

Here, we report a case of an AMI caused by occlusion of a first diagonal branch with subtle ECG changes and negative cardiac biomarkers at presentation, resulting in a delayed diagnosis. From this case, we realized that it is important to compare an initial ECG with serial or prior ECGs to be aware of subtle ECG changes and to repeat cardiac biomarker measurements a few hours later in order not to miss or delay the diagnosis of AMI, when it has not been ruled out.

## Case presentation

A 61-year-old man without medical history presented to our hospital with chest pain that had begun 50 minutes earlier while he was taking a bath in the evening.

## Case presentation

On arrival, the blood pressure was 110/76mmHg, the heart rate 56/min, the oxygen saturation 98% while he was breathing ambient air, and the temperature 36.0℃. Auscultation of the lungs and heart was normal. An ECG showed a sinus rhythm without obvious ST-T change (Figure 1). Chest radiography showed no abnormality (Figure 2). ## Discussion

This case involved a patient with a delayed diagnosis of AMI, despite early hospital arrival after onset. AMI is characterized by myocardial necrosis resulting from an acute occlusion of a coronary artery. Myocardial salvage by immediate reperfusion therapy, including percutaneous coronary angioplasty or fibrinolysis, is critical to limit myocardial necrosis; therefore, delayed diagnosis and treatment could lead to devastating consequences [3]. In our case, two reasons were considered to have contributed to the delayed diagnosis of AMI.

## Discussion

One reason is the subtle ECG changes on the initial ECG. A high lateral wall myocardial infarction caused by occlusion of the first diagonal branch, as in our case, is known that ECG changes could be less apparent [2]. This is because there are no leads that directly show this region, and the distance between the electrodes and this region is relatively far, resulting in small potential changes that are less noticeable in an ECG.

## Discussion

In our case, although the initial ECG obtained 50 minutes after AMI onset seemed to show no obvious ST-T changes, the ECG obtained 11 hours after onset showed a negative T wave in lead aVL only with no obvious change in other leads, and the health checkup ECG obtained 14 months prior also showed a negative T wave in lead aVL. Additionally, by comparison of the initial ECG with the health checkup ECG, ST elevation could be interpreted in lead I on the initial ECG. In this context, lead aVL on the initial ECG could be interpreted as ST elevation rather than an inverted T wave becoming positive, although it did not meet the diagnostic criteria for STEMI: 1mm ST-segment elevation in any two consecutive leads.

## Discussion

AMI has traditionally been classified into ST‑segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and non‑ST‑segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI). STEMI has historically been regarded as a surrogate for occlusion myocardial infarction (OMI), whereas NSTEMI has been considered a surrogate for non‑occlusion myocardial infarction (NOMI). However, approximately half of patients with OMI did not meet STEMI criteria, and 25% to 34% of NSTEMI cases had OMI, resulting in failure of timely reperfusion therapy. Consequently, a new concept of classification of myocardial infarction into OMI versus NOMI (based on pathophysiology) from STEMI versus NSTEMI (based on ST elevation) was proposed. The concept emphasizes whether immediate reperfusion therapy is truly necessary, rather than relying on the conventional STEMI/NSTEMI classification. In this context, ECG has become a key tool for detecting subtle changes suggestive of OMI, and several high‑risk OMI patterns have been identified [4].

## Discussion

In case of isolated first diagonal branch occlusion, 'hyperacute T wave' and 'South Africa flag sign' are known to be high-risk ECG patterns indicative of acute coronary occlusion [4]. In our case, hyperacute T waves appeared to be present in the precordial leads on the initial ECG. However, comparison with the health checkup ECG, as well as the ECGs obtained 11 hours and 35 days after AMI onset, showed minimal change in T‑wave morphology, making true hyperacute T waves unlikely (Figure 7).

## Discussion

The South Africa flag sign is an ECG finding that shows ST elevation in leads I, aVL, and V2, and ST depression in lead III. The distribution of ST segment deviation resembles the pattern of the South African flag in a 3×4 lead display format of ECG. In our case, ST-T changes were only observed in leads I and aVL.

## Discussion

From our case, we realized again that it is important to compare an initial ECG with serial or prior ECGs to be aware of subtle ECG changes in order not to miss or delay the diagnosis of AMI, when it has not been ruled out.

## Discussion

Another reason for the delayed diagnosis is that the cardiac biomarker troponin T was negative at presentation. The 2025 ACC/AHA/ACEP/NAEMSP/SCAI guideline for the management of patients with acute coronary syndrome recommends repeat measurements of conventional troponin T at 3 to 6 hours after initial sampling when acute coronary syndrome has not been ruled out [5]. Although our patient arrived at the hospital just 50 minutes after the onset, AMI was not immediately diagnosed at the time because the initial troponin T was within the normal range. Positive reexamination of troponin T 11 hours after onset led to a high suspicion of AMI; subsequently, an emergent coronary angiogram revealed an occlusion of the first diagonal branch, and percutaneous coronary angioplasty was performed. It had consumed time for diagnosing AMI. If troponin T had been followed up three to six hours after arrival, the result could have become positive, and AMI could have been diagnosed earlier. Despite our patient’s clinical course being uneventful, we realized the importance of serial cardiac biomarker measurements when AMI has not been ruled out.

## Conclusions

We report a case of a high lateral AMI caused by occlusion of a first diagonal branch with subtle ECG changes and negative cardiac biomarkers at presentation, resulting in a delayed diagnosis. We realized that it is important to compare an initial ECG with serial or prior ECGs to be aware of subtle ECG changes and to repeat cardiac biomarker measurements a few hours later in order not to miss or delay the diagnosis of AMI, when it has not been ruled out.
